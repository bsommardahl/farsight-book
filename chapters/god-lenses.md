## God does not see as we see

If we are ever going to learn to see rightly, we have to begin here: God does not merely see more than we see. He sees differently.

That difference is not just a matter of quantity, as if God has a larger information feed than we do. It is a difference of nature, position, purity, authority, and purpose.

We are creatures. He is Creator.
We are located in time. He is eternal.
We are reactive. He is never surprised.
We are self-protective. He is never threatened.
We are morally unstable. He is perfectly holy.
We interpret reality from below, from within confusion, fear, desire, pain, and limitation. God sees reality as it is.

That means His perspective is not one perspective among many. It is the true one.

This is hard for us to absorb because we are always tempted to treat our own experience as the scale of reality. What feels urgent to us feels ultimate. What hurts us feels central. What we cannot understand feels unreasonable. But God's vision is not organized around our panic, our preferences, or our timing.

His nature drives His perspective. And because His nature is true, His perspective is true.

## God is eternal

One of the biggest differences between God's perspective and ours is that God is not trapped inside the tiny slice of reality we call "right now."

We experience life moment by moment, with almost no context. We know a little of the past, almost nothing of the future, and very little even of the present. We react to the fragment in front of us as if it were the whole picture.

God is not like that.

He is eternal. He is not learning in real time. He is not frantically improvising. He is not startled by outcomes, devastated by developments, or forced to revise His goodness because history took an ugly turn. He is never caught off guard because all things exist before Him as the Creator and Lord of all.

That is why what feels like an emergency to us does not feel ultimate to Him.

This does not mean He is indifferent. It means He is not confused.

A small child can skin a knee and feel as if the world has ended. A good father does not mock the pain, but neither does he mistake the pain for the end of the story. He sees more clearly. He knows what matters now, what will pass, what must be learned, and what can be endured.

God's relationship to our earthly emergencies is something like that, only infinitely more wise.

We live as though the present crisis defines reality. God sees the present crisis inside eternity.

## God is Creator, not participant only

God does not merely observe the world from a distance. He is its Maker.

That means He understands reality at the deepest possible level. He knows what things are for. He knows what human beings are for. He knows what love is, what justice requires, what the body is for, what suffering can do, what sin destroys, and what glory awaits.

We, by contrast, often try to define reality from inside our own appetites.

We decide what a good life is by what feels preferable. We decide what is fair by what seems favorable to us. We decide what is meaningful by what gives us relief, recognition, or pleasure. We decide what counts as harm by what disrupts our plans.

But the Creator is not obligated to submit His understanding to the creatures who keep misusing the world He made.

He knows how we work because He made us.

He knows what fulfills our purpose because He gave us that purpose.

He knows what destroys us even when we call it freedom.

He knows what heals us even when we call it loss.

That is one reason God's commands can feel severe to the self-directed heart. They are not suggestions from a fellow struggler. They are truth from the One who knows what human life is actually for.

## God is love

If God's perspective were only power, it would terrify us. If it were only knowledge, it might freeze us. But God's perspective is not cold omniscience. It is shaped by His nature, and Scripture insists that God is love.

That does not mean God is sentimental, permissive, or soft in the way modern people often mean by love.

It means love is not an accessory to His character. It is central.

God created not because He was lacking, but because love overflows. He did not make human beings as machines that could only comply. Real love required the possibility of refusal. Real communion required freedom meaningful enough to reject Him. The risk of rebellion was not a surprise to God. It was a cost He knowingly permitted in a world where real love, real surrender, real fellowship, and real glory would exist.

That changes the way we interpret His perspective.

God is not merely trying to keep order in the universe. He is committed to the increase of holy love. He is committed to bringing people into true fellowship with Himself. He is committed to purifying, correcting, rescuing, forming, and preparing a people fit for His presence.

So when God acts, even in ways we find severe or bewildering, love is not absent. The real question is whether our understanding of love has been reduced to comfort, approval, and immediate relief.

## God is just

Love without justice would not be holy love. It would be indulgence.

One reason people often struggle with God's perspective is that they want a God who is loving in a way that never collides with human preference. But a God who shrugs at evil, excuses rebellion, and treats truth as optional would not be good. He would be weak, false, and morally unserious.

God is not like that.

He is just. He does not misname evil. He does not negotiate with truth. He does not call rebellion beautiful simply because rebels feel sincere. He does not treat sin lightly because sin is not light. It vandalizes what He made. It deforms what He loves. It corrupts judgment, fractures communion, and sets the creature against the Creator.

This is why God's perspective must hold love and justice together.

He sees our rebellion clearly, without euphemism. He also sees our need more deeply than we do. He is not shocked by our sin, but neither is He casual about it. Justice and love are not enemies in Him. They are part of the same holy reality.

We often divide them because our own love is weak and our own justice is self-serving.

God does not have that problem.

## God is never threatened, rushed, or confused

Much of human perspective is shaped by fear.

We rush because we think time is slipping away.
We cling because we think resources are scarce.
We grasp because we think loss will ruin us.
We exaggerate because we think urgency creates safety.
We become controlling because we do not trust what will happen if we let go.

God does not operate from any of those pressures.

He is never threatened. No event can destabilize His rule. No enemy can outmaneuver His wisdom. No human rebellion can corner Him into panic. No delay weakens Him. No suffering surprises Him. No history is outside His reach.

That means God can be patient in ways we cannot imagine.

He can afford to tell the truth slowly, teach through long processes, expose what we would rather leave hidden, and permit seasons that feel unresolved to us. We experience unresolved things as failure. God often uses them as formation.

His patience is not passivity. It is sovereignty without anxiety.

## God sees our lives on the scale of eternity

Human beings think in tiny measurements.

We think in days, deadlines, bills, injuries, insults, promotions, losses, elections, illnesses, arguments, diagnoses, and funerals. Those things are real. They matter. But we consistently mistake their nearness for their total importance.

God sees human life on an entirely different scale.

He sees this world not as the main event but as a brief and meaningful part of a much larger reality. He sees earthly life in relation to eternity. He sees the body in relation to resurrection. He sees suffering in relation to glory. He sees our present loves in relation to what we are becoming forever.

That is why He can value things we regularly undervalue and destabilize things we regularly idolize.

He may care less about preserving our uninterrupted comfort than we do.
He may care more about forming our hearts than we do.
He may care less about our status than we do.
He may care more about our surrender than we do.
He may care less about our earthly image than we do.
He may care more about whether we are becoming capable of real love than we do.

And because He sees eternity clearly, He does not mistake earthly death for ultimate defeat.

Death is terrible. Death is an enemy. But in Christ it is not sovereign. God sees beyond the grave because the grave does not limit His purposes.

## God sees suffering differently than we do

This is one of the hardest places to trust God's perspective.

We tend to divide reality very quickly into categories like pleasant and painful, wanted and unwanted, success and failure, blessing and loss. Once we do that, we assume the interpretation is obvious.

If it hurts, it must be bad.
If it relieves, it must be good.
If it delays my plans, it must be against me.
If it gives me what I want, it must be favor.

But God's perspective is not that shallow.

### Biblical anchor note: Joseph
Joseph is one of scripture’s clearest examples of this. Betrayal, slavery, false accusation, imprisonment, delay — from a human viewpoint, the story looks like waste and injustice stacked on top of each other. But God is not absent in the apparent ruin. He is working a larger purpose that Joseph himself can only see clearly later.

He does not enjoy suffering. He is not careless with wounds. He is not amused by human agony. But neither does He grant pain the authority to define itself.

God can use suffering as discipline, exposure, purification, redirection, mercy, awakening, or preparation. He can use loss to loosen our grip on lesser loves. He can use delay to uncover our demands. He can use weakness to teach dependence. He can use disappointment to rescue us from false gospels we would have kept believing if life had gone smoothly.

Joseph helps us see that God's larger purpose does not make evil good, but it does mean evil does not get the final interpretive word.

This does not answer every question. It does establish one boundary: suffering is not self-interpreting.

And if God's purpose for us is larger than earthly ease, then some of what we call interruption may actually be instruction.

## God sees people more truthfully than we do

Human beings are quick to rank people according to utility, threat, beauty, tribe, productivity, ideology, and social fit.

God does not look at people that way.

He sees image-bearers.
He sees rebels.
He sees the deceived.
He sees the self-righteous.
He sees the lost.
He sees those who are hard to love and still worth loving.
He sees those we call disposable and treats them as morally serious.
He sees the hidden corruption of the respectable and the hidden ache of the obvious sinner.

This is one reason God's perspective so often unsettles religious people.

We want categories that flatter us. We want sin to look worst when it is far from us and obvious in others. We want God to be more offended by the people who embarrass our moral instincts than by the pride, vanity, coldness, greed, and self-protection we cultivate with polished manners.

But God's vision is not fooled by appearances.

He sees the lost not merely as annoyances or enemies, but as people who need rescue.
He sees believers' compromise not as a small private matter, but as wasted time, resisted love, and grief to the Spirit.
He sees every person in relation to the purpose for which He made them.

## God is a relentless teacher

One of the strongest ways to think about God's perspective is to see Him as a wise Father who never wastes a moment.

Not in the sense that every event is easy to decode. Not in the sense that we can always explain what He is doing. But in the sense that nothing is pointless to Him.

He is always teaching.

He teaches through joy and through loss.
He teaches through clarity and through waiting.
He teaches through scripture, through prayer, through rebuke, through disappointment, through beauty, through interruption, through conviction, through the church, through suffering, through mercy.

A nearsighted person wants life mainly managed.
A mature person begins to realize life is being used.

God is not merely trying to get us through our days. He is using our days to form us.

That is why verses like "be still and know that I am God" and "all things work together for good" are often misunderstood. We hear them through a comfort-hungry filter. We assume "good" must mean immediate relief or visible success. But if God's good is our preparation for eternity, our conformity to Christ, and our participation in holy love, then His teaching may take forms we would never have chosen.

## God's purpose for us on earth

God's perspective includes a clear purpose for human life.

We are not here merely to extend our earthly comfort, maximize our options, protect our image, and die with good memories. We are here to know Him, love Him, be formed by Him, and participate in His work of drawing others into that same reality.

To put it simply: God has us here to prepare us for eternity.

That preparation includes salvation, but it does not stop there. It includes sanctification. It includes the renewal of our minds. It includes our becoming people who increasingly agree with Him about what is real, good, urgent, and worth pursuing.

This is why so much of modern Christian confusion feels thin. We keep trying to fit God into a life-plan organized around our own advancement. God keeps working to pull us into a completely different center.

He is not trying to decorate our self-importance. He is trying to free us from it.

## God's perspective is the healing of ours

At first, God's perspective can feel threatening.

It is too large.
Too holy.
Too unconcerned with our little vanities.
Too honest about our rebellion.
Too calm in the face of what we call emergencies.
Too patient when we want speed.
Too severe where we want indulgence.
Too demanding where we want affirmation.

But this is exactly why His perspective is our hope.

If God saw as poorly as we do, we would be lost.
If He reacted as rashly as we do, we would be consumed.
If He loved as shallowly as we do, we would never be changed.
If He judged as selectively as we do, there would be no justice.
If He valued comfort as highly as we do, we would remain spiritually childish forever.

The fact that God sees differently is not the threat. It is the rescue.

His perspective is not given to humiliate us for being creatures. It is given to heal the blindness that keeps us trapped in ourselves.

And that means the right response to God's perspective is not argument from below. It is humility.

Not blind irrationality, but creaturely humility. The humility to admit that reality is not measured by our feelings, our fears, our politics, our appetites, or our pain.

Reality is measured by God.

And because He is loving, just, eternal, wise, and true, learning to see as He sees is not the loss of ourselves. It is the recovery of our sanity.

## Story options

### Primary biblical story choice
- **Joseph**
  - strongest overall fit for the chapter
  - suffering, delay, injustice, providence, and purpose all converge naturally
  - shows how God can be working a larger story while human beings see only fragments

### Secondary biblical story choices
- **Jesus with sinners, sufferers, and the self-righteous** — strongest for how God sees people differently than we do
- **Jericho, if handled carefully** — strongest for destabilizing shallow assumptions about love and justice, but high-risk and not necessary early

### Primary human story choice
- **a testimony where what felt catastrophic became formative**
  - best fit for embodying the chapter’s argument that God sees beyond immediate pain
  - helps keep the theology from becoming too elevated or abstract

### Strong alternate human story angle
- a parent/child perspective story showing limited understanding from below

### Ordinary/example angle to weave throughout
- child wanting to inspect the parent’s plan
- skinned-knee versus end-of-world analogy
- urgent crisis shrinking when seen on a larger timeline

### Current recommendation
Use:
- Joseph as the governing biblical frame
- one suffering story later seen differently in hindsight
- everyday parent/child and time-scale analogies to keep the chapter grounded
