## Why blur feels normal

Most people do not realize they are looking through a lens until something cleaner is set beside it.

Human beings are like that spiritually. We do not merely have opinions about life. We have ways of seeing that were formed long before we started naming them. We call them wisdom, common sense, realism, discernment, instinct, or just “the way life is.” But many of those so-called instincts are simply familiar distortions.

That matters because nearsightedness rarely feels like nearsightedness from the inside. It feels like realism. It feels like maturity. It feels like prudence. It feels like common sense.

If the previous chapter named the problem, this chapter explains why the problem feels so normal. We do not naturally see reality from nowhere. We see it through the accumulated influence of creaturely limitation, fear, pain, desire, pride, culture, and self-protection.

In other words, blurry vision comes naturally to us.

We interpret life through human lenses before we ever learn to interpret life through God's perspective. Those lenses are not neutral. They are fallen, limited, emotionally charged, and deeply shaped by survival, pain, social formation, and self-interest.

This chapter is therefore not mainly about condemning people. It is about explaining why distorted sight feels so normal from the inside.

## We are creatures, not neutral observers

We do not wake up seeing reality clearly. We wake up inside a body, inside time, inside need, inside limitation. We do not choose to begin as neutral observers floating above reality. We begin as creatures.

That does not make us wicked by itself. It does make us profoundly unqualified to treat our first instincts as the measure of truth.

We know what it is like to be hungry, tired, threatened, embarrassed, attracted, overlooked, afraid, and in pain. We know what it is like to live inside a small slice of time with very little perspective. We react to what is near, loud, immediate, and emotionally charged. Then we are tempted to call those reactions wisdom.

But proximity is not the same as importance. Urgency is not the same as ultimacy. Our creaturely experience, while real, is far too partial to function as the final scale of reality.

This is one reason human beings confuse what is close with what is central. We feel the pressure of the immediate moment and quietly assume that whatever is most vivid to us must also be most decisive. But a creature's felt nearness to something does not mean that creature sees the thing truly.

We never see reality from nowhere. We see it as limited creatures inside time, weakness, ignorance, and desire. Unless God teaches us otherwise, we will keep mistaking our angle of vision for reality itself.

## Survival teaches us to narrow everything

### Main point
Self-preservation trains us to prioritize threat, comfort, security, and immediate outcomes.

### Ideas to include
- survival is not evil, but it can become interpretive tyranny
- fear shrinks vision
- self-protection makes us reactive and controlling
- parental/protector instincts can become distorted if not governed by God

### Useful lines / source ideas
- self preservation, survival
- parent instincts, protector
- our problems are the end of the world
- we seek control because we feel vulnerable

### Candidate A/B contrast
- Which is better? A life organized around self-protection, or a life surrendered to God’s purposes?

### Biblical anchor note: Peter rebuking Jesus (Matthew 16)
Peter is one of the best biblical examples for this section. He is sincere, loyal, and emotionally engaged, yet when Jesus speaks about suffering and the cross, Peter immediately answers from a human protective instinct: “This shall never happen to you.” From Peter’s perspective, that response feels loving, sensible, and strong. But it is still human vision opposing divine purpose.

### Expanded draft
A threatened person does not ask first what is true, holy, or eternal. A threatened person asks what is safest.

That is one reason fear is such a powerful lens. It can make temporary danger feel ultimate and can make obedience feel irresponsible. It can make control look like wisdom. It can make self-protection look like maturity. It can make trust look naive.

Peter's rebuke of Jesus shows this with painful clarity. Jesus speaks of the suffering to come, and Peter responds as many of us would: protect, avoid, prevent, preserve. It sounds loving. It sounds reasonable. It sounds like common sense. But Jesus identifies it as human thinking set against the things of God.

Survival is not evil. The instinct to protect life, family, and well-being is part of creaturely existence. But once self-preservation becomes the ruling filter, our sight shrinks. We begin interpreting everything by the question of immediate risk. We look first for threat, not truth; for relief, not reality.

That is how fear trains nearsightedness. It narrows the field until whatever feels dangerous seems final.

## Pain, history, and trauma color what we see

No one sees with untouched eyes.

We bring memories into every room. We bring family history into prayer. We bring betrayal into trust. We bring loss into our expectations of God. We bring old disappointments into new situations and then wonder why our reactions are so strong.

Pain is especially powerful because it teaches quickly. A person who has been wounded often learns to scan for repetition. A person who has lost deeply often learns to brace for more loss. A person who has been humiliated often learns to protect image before truth. Trauma and disappointment can train us to call defense wisdom because defense once helped us survive.

This does not mean wounded people are irrational. It means scars speak. And unless God retrains us, we may go on treating pain-shaped instincts as if they were trustworthy guides to reality.

## Culture and upbringing train our defaults

Before most people ever read theology, they have already been discipled by family, fear, ambition, grievance, entertainment, and national myth. Then they come to scripture and assume they are reading it plainly, when in fact they are reading it through ten thousand inherited assumptions.

We inherit ways of seeing before we ever evaluate them. Family systems train assumptions about success, safety, conflict, worth, affection, and power. Culture catechizes us constantly in entitlement, status, productivity, comfort, autonomy, and image. Then we rename those inherited instincts "common sense" and barely notice that we are doing it.

This is one reason conversion does not instantly free a person from distorted sight. A Christian may sincerely love God and still spend years confusing cultural assumptions with biblical wisdom. They may think they are reading scripture plainly when they are actually reading it through the lens of ambition, grievance, nationalism, class anxiety, or comfort.

Human lenses are communal as well as personal. We do not invent them alone. We absorb them.

## Desire makes us call blurry vision realistic

### Main point
We rarely want the truth in the abstract. We usually want a version of truth that leaves our preferred life intact.

### Ideas to include

- comfort, pleasure, wealth, image, autonomy, and recognition distort sight
- we call what we want “reasonable”
- we resist truths that threaten our preferred identities

### Useful lines / source ideas
- desires
- putting God first but not practically
- abundance / prosperity confusion
- life-ism, materialism, entertainment
- we want God to work more in health and finances than in our hearts

### Candidate A/B contrast

### Stub
Desire does not merely pull us toward things. It edits our vision. It makes the wanted seem wise, the inconvenient seem extreme, and the costly seem unnecessary. This is why people can call themselves surrendered while fiercely protecting the exact parts of life they least want God to touch.

## Pride keeps us from suspecting our own sight

One of the deepest problems with human lenses is that we usually trust them. We do not just see poorly. We defend our poor sight.

Pride is not only arrogance. Often it is confidence in our own scale, our own reading, our own instincts, and our own centrality. It says, “My judgments are fair. My reactions make sense. My interpretation is reality.”

That is why pride can survive inside very religious people. Knowledge can deepen pride rather than heal it. Familiarity with Christian language can make a person more certain of their own sight without making that sight any clearer. They can become harder to correct precisely because they now have spiritual vocabulary with which to defend themselves.

Pride keeps us from suspecting our own lens. It would rather explain, justify, accuse, or perform than pause long enough to ask whether the problem may be in the way we are seeing.

## Why human lenses make God hard to understand

Many of our theological struggles are not purely intellectual. They are perceptual.

We are trying to understand God while insisting on using a lens that puts self-preservation, immediate comfort, and earthly success at the center. From inside that lens, of course God often looks difficult.

If comfort is central, His severity feels unfair. If immediate symmetry is our definition of fairness, His patience with evil and His strange uses of suffering feel confusing. If control is sacred, trust feels dangerous. If this life is the main event, then His refusal to organize everything around our ease can make Him seem distant, harsh, or disappointing.

Often the issue is not that God is unclear. It is that we are trying to read Him through a lens that was never fit to judge Him.

## Human lenses are understandable, but not trustworthy

### Main point
Human lenses are understandable, but they are not trustworthy enough to govern a life.

### Goals of closing section
- show compassion without excusing distortion
- prepare the reader to want God’s perspective more deeply
- set up the God-lenses chapter clearly

### Stub
There is a reason human vision feels natural. It was formed by weakness, time, fear, longing, memory, and pride. But natural is not the same as true. Familiar is not the same as clear.

If our default lens is this compromised, then the next question is obvious: what does true sight actually look like?

We do not just need to understand ourselves better. We need to see how God sees.

## Story options

### Primary biblical story choice
- **Peter rebuking Jesus (Matthew 16)**
  - strongest fit for the chapter’s central argument
  - shows a sincere disciple seeing through a deeply human, protective lens
  - “This shall never happen to you” captures how human realism can oppose divine purpose while still feeling loving and reasonable

### Secondary biblical story choices
- **Job’s friends** — strongest for inherited theology, simplistic fairness, and bad readings of suffering
- **Samuel and David (1 Samuel 16)** — strongest for showing how instinct judges by externals while God sees differently

### Primary human story choice
- **a trauma-shaped reaction story showing defense becoming a lens**
  - best fit for explaining why distorted sight feels normal rather than merely sinful
  - should be compassionate and specific, not dramatic for its own sake

### Strong alternate human story angle
- a parenting/protector story showing how fear can narrow judgment while still feeling like wisdom

### Ordinary/example angle to weave throughout
- fear making control look wise
- betrayal making trust feel foolish
- comfort making obedience seem irresponsible

### Current recommendation
Use:
- Peter rebuking Jesus as the governing biblical story
- one compassionate human story about pain shaping perception
- ordinary examples throughout to keep the chapter from feeling too abstract
