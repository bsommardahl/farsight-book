## We do not see as clearly as we think

Most Christians would never say out loud that this life is all that matters.

We know the right words. We say eternity matters. We say heaven is real. We say God is in control. We say this world is not our home.

But then trouble shows up, and what we actually believe starts leaking out.

A diagnosis changes the tone of the week and suddenly God feels far away. Money gets tight and panic takes over as if the kingdom of God has collapsed along with our budget. Someone inconveniences us and, instead of seeing a person made in the image of God, we see an interruption. We talk about eternal life, but we grieve earthly death as if it were absolute defeat. We say Jesus is enough, but live as if comfort, safety, reputation, and control are the things we really cannot lose.

That is not merely inconsistency. It is impaired vision.

This is one of the great spiritual problems of the Christian life: we claim to belong to an eternal kingdom while continuing to interpret reality through a temporary lens.

We are nearsighted.

## Nearsighted Christianity

Nearsighted Christianity is not simply bad behavior. It is what happens when believers interpret life mainly through the categories of earthbound experience: survival, comfort, approval, success, pain avoidance, fairness, and immediate outcomes.

A nearsighted Christian may still be sincere. He may still pray, serve, give, attend church, and speak Christian language fluently. But underneath all of that, his reactions reveal that his field of vision is still painfully small.

He experiences problems only as threats, not also as places where God may be teaching, exposing, pruning, or redirecting. He sees death mainly as tragedy, not also as transition. He sees other people mainly in terms of how they affect his peace, progress, status, or comfort. He reads scripture looking for techniques to improve life now rather than for revelation that teaches him how to see reality as God sees it.

This kind of vision problem is common enough that it can feel normal. In many churches, it practically is normal. We have learned how to sound eternal while living temporal.

## What nearsightedness looks like

Spiritual nearsightedness has evidence.

It shows up when we treat this life as the main event instead of the short introduction to eternity. It shows up when we act as though death is the ultimate disaster, suffering is automatically meaningless, and comfort is something God owes us. It shows up when we measure blessing by convenience, answered prayer by immediate relief, and Christian maturity by visible rule-keeping.

It shows up when people become obstacles.

That is one of the clearest signs. If my first instinct is to sort people into categories like useful, annoying, threatening, embarrassing, needy, or in my way, then I am not seeing them clearly. I may be seeing them efficiently. I may be seeing them socially. I may be seeing them strategically. But I am not seeing them as God sees them.

It also shows up in the way we think about sin.

Nearsighted people usually treat sin in one of two shallow ways. Either sin is a list of behaviors that might get us in trouble, or sin is a manageable weakness we plan to carry indefinitely while still thinking well of ourselves. But sin is more serious than that. Sin is evidence of disorder in the heart. It is rebellion, yes, but it is also blindness. It is the childish insistence that our own instincts are trustworthy enough to guide our lives.

And because our vision is distorted, even our religion becomes distorted. We want salvation as a box to check. We want forgiveness without surrender. We want spiritual power without transformation. We want God near enough to solve our problems but not so near that He exposes what we love more than Him.

## How we misread the Christian life

When our vision is temporal, we misread almost everything.

When we hear that God is with us, we assume it means He will make this life easier, smoother, safer, or more successful. So when obedience becomes costly or painful, we feel betrayed, because we quietly expected divine assistance with our earthly agenda.

When we read about the fruit of the Spirit, we often turn it into a list of traits we are supposed to imitate with enough discipline, rather than seeing it as evidence of what the Spirit produces in a person whose inner world is being changed.

When we read about sin, we reduce it to behavior management. We focus on staying out of trouble, preserving our image, or avoiding consequences. We do not naturally see sin as proof that parts of us are still resisting God's transforming work.

When we read the Old Testament, we often treat it like a pile of disconnected stories from a strange religious past. We miss the long, patient evidence of God preparing a people, revealing His character, confronting human rebellion, and moving history toward a much larger end than earthly stability.

When we hear Jesus speak of life, abundance, treasure, reward, the kingdom, and salvation, we drag all of those words down into the size of our present appetites. We hear them through the ears of people who are still obsessed with comfort. We assume "abundant life" must mean a better earthly experience because that is what we would choose if the choice were ours.

That is how nearsightedness works. It shrinks everything to the scale of now.

## The danger of nominal sight

This is where nominal Christianity becomes especially dangerous.

A person can learn the language of faith without ever surrendering his categories. He can say "God is good," "Jesus saves," "the Bible is true," and "everything happens for a reason," while still evaluating every situation like a practical atheist. His words sound Christian, but his assumptions remain worldly.

He still thinks the good life means safety, success, admiration, comfort, and control. He still thinks the worst thing that can happen is earthly loss. He still thinks other people's public sins are more offensive than his own hidden self-worship. He still treats obedience as a strategy for getting better outcomes rather than as a response of love, surrender, and trust.

That is why nominal Christianity can be harder to expose than open unbelief. Open unbelief is at least honest about its center. Nominal Christianity hides self-rule under religious vocabulary.

This is not a call to obsess over whether every struggling Christian is fake. That would just be another form of blindness. Weak believers are real. Confused believers are real. Wounded believers are real. The issue is not whether Christians ever struggle. The issue is whether we are content to keep seeing life through the same tiny lens year after year while calling that maturity.

## Our reactions reveal our theology

What we say we believe matters, but what we instinctively do with suffering, delay, temptation, interruption, and loss reveals even more.

Our reactions tell the truth on us.

If every inconvenience feels like a personal attack, maybe I think my comfort is sacred.

If every unanswered prayer makes me suspicious of God's goodness, maybe I have defined goodness in purely earthly terms.

If every hardship feels meaningless unless I can explain it immediately, maybe I think understanding is a prerequisite for trust.

If the possibility of losing status, money, health, pleasure, or control terrifies me more than hardness of heart, maybe I am still calling this present life "ultimate" even while singing songs about heaven.

If I am enraged by the public sin of unbelievers but casual about my own pride, greed, vanity, coldness, and self-protection, then I am not seeing clearly. I am measuring sin politically, socially, and aesthetically, not spiritually.

This is one reason spiritual formation can be measured by recovery time. How long does it take us to come back to our senses? How long does it take us to remember that God is God, this life is short, eternity is real, and love matters more than our temporary agitation?

A nearsighted person may eventually remember the truth. But he keeps forgetting it as soon as life gets loud.

## Why this matters so much

If nearsightedness only made us slightly less peaceful, it would still be a problem. But it does more than that.

It distorts our understanding of God.
It distorts our understanding of ourselves.
It distorts our understanding of salvation.
It distorts our understanding of suffering.
It distorts our understanding of what love is for.
It distorts our understanding of why we are here.

And once those things are distorted, we start building an entire Christian life around the wrong center.

We ask the wrong questions.
We pursue the wrong goals.
We fear the wrong losses.
We celebrate the wrong victories.
We measure growth by the wrong standards.

We may still be very active. We may still be very moral. We may still be very "involved." But active blindness is still blindness.

That is why this problem cannot be solved by trying harder to behave. Better performance does not heal distorted sight. We need more than correction. We need renewal.

## The problem beneath the problem

At the bottom of spiritual nearsightedness is a simple but devastating confusion: we keep assuming that the purpose of the Christian life is to help us live well here.

Not always in crude prosperity-gospel ways. Often in much subtler forms.

We want Christianity to make us safer, calmer, cleaner, more stable, more respectable, more functional, and more successful. If it also gets us into heaven later, even better.

But if God's purpose is actually to prepare us for eternity, form us in love, strip away our illusions, and teach us to see as He sees, then much of what we call disappointment may actually be discipleship.

Much of what feels like loss may be mercy.
Much of what feels like delay may be formation.
Much of what feels like exposure may be love.

That does not make pain unreal. It does mean pain is not self-interpreting.

## We need a different lens

The answer to nearsightedness is not pretending pain does not hurt. It is not becoming emotionally numb. It is not learning to say more spiritual-sounding sentences.

The answer is learning to see differently.

Before we can understand what it means to see with farsight, we have to admit that we have been seeing with human, temporal, self-protective lenses for a very long time. We have been treating our instincts as wisdom. We have been assuming our scale is the right one. We have been shrinking God, the kingdom, suffering, purpose, and even salvation down to dimensions we can manage.

That is why the next question is not merely what is wrong with us morally.

The next question is: **why do human beings see this way in the first place?**

## Story options

### Primary biblical story choice
- **Rich young ruler (Mark 10)**
  - strongest fit for the chapter’s central diagnosis
  - moral seriousness, religious language, and real blindness all appear together
  - exposes how eternal invitation can be refused because temporal attachment still rules

### Secondary biblical story choices
- **Martha and Mary (Luke 10)** — strongest for distorted urgency and misordered importance
- **Jonah** — strongest for partial obedience and distorted vision toward other people

### Primary human story choice
- **a believer panicking over money, health, or status while still speaking Christian language**
  - best fit for showing nearsightedness as common, familiar, and revealing
  - works better than a dramatic story because the chapter needs recognizability more than spectacle

### Strong alternate human story angle
- a moral-failure story that exposes image management versus real transformation

### Ordinary/example angle to weave throughout
- diagnosis changing the tone of an entire week
- inconvenience turning a person into an obstacle
- public sin in others feeling outrageous while private pride feels normal

### Current recommendation
Use:
- rich young ruler near the center of the chapter
- a familiar panic/control story early
- ordinary examples throughout so the whole chapter feels uncomfortably close to home
